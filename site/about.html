Catena

A visualization of the web of tradition — the connected net of citations within the papal encyclicals, and the councils, saints, and scripture they draw upon.

The golden chain

In the thirteenth century, Thomas Aquinas compiled the Catena Aurea — the "golden chain" — a commentary on the four Gospels assembled entirely from the words of the Church Fathers. Verse by verse, he strung together quotation after quotation, Greek and Latin, so that the continuous witness of tradition could be read as a single unbroken chain. Nothing was invented; everything was linked.

Catena takes up that same instinct for the modern magisterium. Every encyclical is woven from those before it. When Leo XIV promulgated Magnifica Humanitas in 2026, he hung 224 footnotes upon prior encyclicals, ecumenical councils, the writings of saints, papal homilies, and the words of scripture. Each citation is a link in a chain reaching back across the centuries. This project makes those links visible.

What it shows

The goal is to catalog and render the interconnectedness of the Catholic intellectual tradition — to let you see the conversation a document is having with its forebears. Catena reads each source text, extracts every reference, resolves them to the works and authors they name, and draws the result as a single living constellation: documents and the citations that bind them, the people and councils who wrote them, and the scripture they return to again and again.

How to read the constellation

Each point of light is one of three things:

  • Works — encyclicals, council documents, exhortations, and the writings of the saints. The largest, brightest one is the encyclical in view; a work's size grows with how often it is cited.
  • Authors & councils — the popes, saints, ecumenical councils, and dicasteries who stand behind those works.
  • Scripture — the biblical passages the tradition cites, the oldest links in the chain.

A thread between two points is a citation. Where the Catena Aurea presents its chain as a column of linked quotations, Catena presents it as a graph: hover any node to trace its connections, and click to pin a node. Pin two or more and the graph reveals the works that bridge them — for instance, selecting an encyclical and a Church Father surfaces precisely the writings of that Father the encyclical leans on. The detail panel always describes whatever you last clicked, and the search box (or the / key) will find and fly to any work, author, or passage.

Sources & method

Primary texts are drawn from vatican.va, with papalencyclicals.net as an archival fallback for older documents. A small pipeline fetches each text (politely, and cached), parses its footnotes into structured citations, and collapses every language edition and surface spelling of a work onto a single identity, so the same document is one link in the chain no matter how it is named. Catena is a reverent index, not a substitute: every node links back to the original text.

What comes next

Today the graph centers on a single encyclical and the works it cites. The chain grows in every direction: parsing the works those documents cite in turn, until the constellation spans the whole multi-generational tradition; an adapter for the older texts; and tools to trace a single idea's lineage across the centuries.
